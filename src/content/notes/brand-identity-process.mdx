The business had exported garments internationally for years with no visual identity to speak of. No logo. No defined palette. Business cards were plain text on white cardstock, and correspondence went out unbranded. When international buyers evaluated the company alongside competitors, first impressions were doing quiet damage.

I had no formal design training. I had a good eye, reference material from established brands, and the willingness to study what worked before touching anything. This is what the process taught me.

## Research before pixels

Before opening a design tool, I spent days studying how garment exporters in Bangladesh, Turkey and Vietnam presented themselves, and how established fashion supply chain companies structured their brand systems. The pattern was consistent: clean typography, restrained palettes, professional photography. The best brands looked trustworthy, and for a manufacturer, trustworthy is the entire objective.

That research became the brief. Without it, the identity would have been decoration.

## What the identity included

The delivered system covered every touchpoint the business actually used:

- a logo system with primary, secondary and icon variations
- a color palette with usage rules and print-ready values
- typography selections for headings, body text and accents
- business cards, letterheads, trim cards and compliment slips
- a company profile PDF used in buyer presentations
- brand guidelines documenting all of it

The company profile became the most used artifact in the set. It appears in buyer meetings to this day.

## Consistency beats cleverness

The biggest lesson: a simple identity applied consistently across every touchpoint builds more trust than a clever one applied inconsistently. Buyers see the letterhead, the card, the profile and the website as one entity. Every inconsistency, a different blue, a stretched logo, reads as disorganization.

So the guidelines document matters as much as the logo. The design work is only half the deliverable; the other half is making sure the identity survives contact with real use.

## Design for the context

A trim card travels inside a garment box and must survive shipping. A letterhead gets printed on an office printer, not a press. A company profile is read on a laptop screen during a meeting. Designing for how each artifact is actually used changes the decisions: bleed margins, contrast, file formats.

The general lesson applies well beyond branding: know the environment your work will live in before you shape it.

## Start with typography

When I began, I reached for the logo first. That was backwards. Choosing the typefaces first, a professional serif for headings and a clean sans for body, set the register for everything else. The palette, the spacing, even the logo's construction fell into place around the typographic voice.

Typography is the identity's skeleton. Build it first.
